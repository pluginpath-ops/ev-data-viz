## The short version

An EV's battery pack comes in one of two broad voltage classes, called
"400 V" and "800 V". The names are a class, not a reading. The iX3's
"800 V" pack is rated 698.9 V [[fact:bmw-ix3-voltage]]. The Gravity's is
quoted at 926 V by Lucid and listed at 810 V by the EPA
[[fact:lucid-gravity-voltage]]. A pack's voltage moves with its charge, and
makers quote different points on that range: Lucid's figure is near full,
the EPA's near nominal [[fact:gravity-voltage-points]]. Even the EPA's own
data mixes them from one maker to the next [[fact:epa-voltage-points-differ]]. EVBench files both as
800 V class.

The "400 V" class reaches lower than its name. GM's 85 kWh-class Ultium
cars, the Equinox EV among them, run about 288 V nominal and under 270 V
when low [[fact:gm-ultium-80s-pack]].

No standard defines the two classes; they are shorthand
[[fact:pack-voltage-class-ranges]]. EVBench draws the line at 475 V
nominal: below it a pack is 400 V class [[fact:evbench-voltage-class-split]].
That is about the highest nominal voltage that can still charge on a
charger that tops out at 500 V.

Higher voltage means less current for the same power
[[fact:physics-current-for-power]]. The Cybertruck shows it in one car,
because it charges both ways: its pack splits into two halves for
lower-voltage chargers [[fact:cybertruck-400v-method]]. The pack is about
710 V, so each half is about 355 V [[fact:cybertruck-voltage]].

- On a V4 post fed by an older V3 cabinet, which tops out at 500 V
  [[fact:older-chargers-500v]], the truck switches its pack into two halves
  and charges them at 355 V. Tesla enabled up to 325 kW there
  [[fact:cybertruck-kw-by-charger]], which takes about **915 A**. The V4
  post's heavier cable is what allows it; the cabinet behind it was never
  the limit [[fact:v4-post-lifts-v3-cabinet]].
- On a V4 cabinet that reaches 1000 V [[fact:tesla-v4-cabinet-voltage]], it
  charges as one 710 V pack. 500 kW [[fact:cybertruck-500kw]] takes about
  **700 A**: half again the power, on a quarter less current.

*Those currents are derived from power ÷ voltage, not measured*
[[fact:physics-cybertruck-currents]]. Current is what heats cables and
connectors, so the higher-voltage configuration takes more power through
the same cable.

The same arithmetic works against a low-voltage pack. At a charger's 500 A
limit, a pack at 288 V takes at most 144 kW, where one at 400 V takes
200 kW [[fact:physics-low-voltage-cap]]. In EVBench's experience, the
smaller Ultium cars do charge slowly even on 500 A chargers
[[fact:gm-ultium-slow-at-500a]].

Voltage does **not** make the cells themselves charge faster. That is the
part most often gotten wrong. The next section explains why.

## What changes, and what doesn't

Think of voltage as water pressure and current as flow rate.[^1] Doubling
the pressure moves the same power with half the flow, so the pipes (the
cable, the charge port, the contactors, the fuses) can be smaller or run
cooler.

The cells are a different story. To build an 800 V pack, a maker connects
about twice as many cells in series. For the same pack energy, that leaves
half as many in parallel. So at any given charging power, **each cell
carries the same current it would in a 400 V pack of the same energy.**
Each cell makes the same heat and runs into the same chemistry limits.
Voltage raises the ceiling set by the wiring. It does not move the limits
set by the cells.

::: engineers
- Pack: S cells in series × P in parallel. V_pack ≈ S × V_cell and
  E_pack ≈ S × P × V_cell × Ah_cell. At fixed E_pack, doubling S halves P.
- Per-cell current: I_cell = I_pack / P = (kW / V_pack) / P. Doubling
  V_pack halves I_pack and halves P, so I_cell does not change.
- Heat in the cells is I_cell² × R_cell per cell, the same count of cells,
  so it is the same total. Heat in the conductors *outside* the cells is
  I_pack² × R_conductor, which drops to a quarter at the same conductor
  resistance.
- So an 800 V pack's advantage is in the parts that carry pack current:
  the cable and connector, contactors, fuses, busbars and HV harness. It is
  not in the cells.

:::

## Where this shows up in EVBench

- The iX3 peaks at 400 kW on an 800 V charger
  [[fact:bmw-ix3-peak-kw]]. The Gravity peaks at 400 kW on 1000 V equipment
  [[fact:lucid-gravity-peak-kw]]. Both are 800 V class.
- The price of 800 V is the chargers still installed that cannot reach
  that voltage. In the US these are mainly older 50 kW units and
  Superchargers before V4 [[fact:older-chargers-500v]]. See [How 800 V cars use 400 V chargers](400v-charger-compatibility).
- High voltage has a ceiling too. Lucid quotes the Gravity's pack at
  926 V, above an ABB Terra 360's 920 V maximum [[fact:gravity-above-terra360]],
  and in EVBench's experience Lucids can struggle on chargers rated
  920–950 V [[fact:lucid-high-voltage-chargers]].

---

[^1]: *Where it breaks:* in a pipe, the losses do not grow with the square
of the flow. In a wire they do: resistive heat is I²R, so halving the
current cuts the cable's heat to a quarter, not a half. That square is the
whole reason voltage matters here.
